Purpose
The purpose of this document is to define the Defense Industrial Base (DIB) sector as a formal, independent sector within the CAI USA framework. The DIB sector is treated as a first-class sector due to its direct impact on national defense, military readiness, controlled information environments, sensitive supply chains, and exposure to advanced and persistent cyber threats. This document establishes the scope, assumptions, constraints, and operational posture required to apply CAI coherently within DIB environments while preserving national security relevance, accountability, and regulatory neutrality.

Sector Scope
The Defense Industrial Base sector under CAI USA encompasses organizations, systems, and environments involved in the research, design, development, manufacturing, integration, maintenance, sustainment, and support of defense-related systems, technologies, and services. This includes prime contractors, subcontractors, suppliers, research entities, test environments, manufacturing systems, enterprise IT, OT and industrial environments, cloud services, and interconnected partner systems whose compromise could impact national security, defense capabilities, or trust relationships. This sector definition intentionally avoids defining contractual, regulatory, or certification processes, which remain external to CAI.

Sector Assumptions
Defense Industrial Base environments operate under heightened confidentiality, integrity, and availability constraints. Controlled information and sensitive intellectual property are central assets. Adversaries are assumed to be well-resourced, persistent, and capable of multi-stage operations including supply-chain compromise, insider exploitation, and long-term stealthy presence. Human authority and governance are mandatory for high-impact actions. Automation must be deterministic, observable, reversible, and auditable. Cybersecurity actions must preserve evidence, attribution potential, and legal accountability.

Operational Characteristics
DIB systems exhibit long asset lifecycles, heterogeneous technologies, and complex trust relationships across organizational and national boundaries. Supply chains are deep, global, and difficult to fully control. Manufacturing and OT environments may coexist with enterprise IT and cloud platforms. Downtime tolerance varies by system but uncontrolled disruption is unacceptable. Incident response must balance containment with intelligence collection, legal obligations, and operational continuity.

Threat and Risk Context
Threats relevant to the DIB sector include espionage, intellectual property theft, sabotage, integrity manipulation, supply-chain compromise, credential abuse, and long-term persistence by advanced adversaries. Threat modeling prioritizes strategic impact, cascading consequences, and trust degradation over simple frequency metrics. Assumed breach is treated conservatively, with emphasis on observation, containment, evidence preservation, and controlled response.

Relationship to CAI CORE
This sector definition inherits all semantics, lifecycle stages, principles, integrity governance mechanisms, and evolution controls defined in CAI CORE. The seven-stage CAI lifecycle is applied without modification. CAI CORE remains authoritative for taxonomy, execution semantics, contamination prevention, and governance. This document does not redefine CAI concepts and must remain consistent with CAI-Core-Definition, CAI-Core-Architecture, CAI-Core-Operations, CAI-Core-Operational-State-Model, and CAI-Core-Standards-Evolution-and-Governance.

Relationship to CAI ENGINE
The Defense Industrial Base sector is executed through the CAI Engine under strict governance, safety, and accountability constraints. Automation and orchestration are bounded by policy enforcement, safety gates, and mandatory human oversight. The CAI Engine enforces execution discipline but does not override organizational authority, contractual obligations, or national security decision processes.

Relationship to CAI USA Baseline
This sector definition extends the CAI USA baseline without redefining it. It operates alongside CAI-USA-Definition, CAI-USA-CCES, CAI-USA-Telemetry-and-Data-Model, CAI-USA-Secure-by-Design-Controls, CAI-USA-AI-Governance-and-Assurance, CAI-USA-ICS-OT-Safety-Gating, CAI-USA-Threat-Model-Integration, and CAI-USA-Traceability-Matrix. Explicit Non-Claims
This document does not assert compliance with DoD, CMMC, DFARS, NIST, CISA, DOJ, or any other authority. It does not certify systems, authorize operational changes, or replace contractual, regulatory, or legal processes. It provides a structured cybersecurity operational framework only.
