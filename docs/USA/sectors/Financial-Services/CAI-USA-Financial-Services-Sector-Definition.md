## Purpose

The purpose of this document is to define the Financial Services sector as a formal, independent, first-class sector within the CAI USA framework.  
Financial Services environments are uniquely sensitive to trust, integrity, availability, and systemic risk. Cyber incidents in this sector may propagate rapidly across organizations, markets, and national economies, producing cascading impacts beyond the originating entity.

This document establishes the authoritative scope, assumptions, constraints, and operational posture required to apply CAI coherently within Financial Services environments while preserving CAI CORE semantics, USA baseline governance, and strict non-claim positioning.

## Sector Scope

The Financial Services sector under CAI USA encompasses systems, environments, and operational domains involved in the creation, movement, storage, settlement, clearing, verification, and trust management of monetary value.

This includes, but is not limited to:

- Retail, commercial, and investment banking systems
- Payment processing and clearing infrastructures
- Interbank transfer and settlement systems
- Capital markets, exchanges, and trading platforms
- Digital payment services and financial technology platforms
- Identity, authentication, authorization, and trust services supporting financial operations
- Supporting IT, cloud, analytics, and data platforms whose compromise could affect financial integrity, availability, or confidence

This sector definition intentionally avoids defining regulatory compliance, supervisory authority, licensing, or legal enforcement processes, which remain external to CAI.

## Sector Assumptions

Financial Services environments operate under continuous availability, integrity, and trust constraints.  
Transactions are assumed to be time-sensitive, non-repudiable, and legally consequential.  
Human authority remains mandatory for actions that may materially impact financial outcomes, systemic stability, or legal accountability.  
Automation and AI may support detection, prioritization, and analysis but must remain governed, explainable, and reversible.  
Cybersecurity actions must not introduce uncontrolled financial loss, integrity ambiguity, or irreversible transactional states.  
Evidence generation, traceability, and accountability are mandatory due to legal, regulatory, and investigative scrutiny.

## Operational Characteristics

Financial Services systems exhibit highly interconnected transaction flows and dependency chains.  
Operational latency tolerances are low, and downtime has immediate economic and reputational consequences.  
Trust relationships extend across organizations, third-party service providers, and national boundaries.  
Automated and AI-assisted systems are commonly used for fraud detection, risk scoring, and transaction monitoring.  
Incident response must preserve transactional integrity, evidence fidelity, and accountability while minimizing systemic disruption.

## Threat and Risk Context

Threats relevant to the Financial Services sector include transaction manipulation, fraud, identity compromise, abuse of automated decision systems, integrity attacks against financial records, availability disruption, and supply-chain compromise of critical service providers.

Threat modeling in this sector prioritizes systemic impact, trust erosion, and cascading economic effects over isolated technical exploitation.  
Assumed breach is treated conservatively, with containment and observation prioritized over aggressive eviction where financial integrity or continuity may be affected.

## Relationship to CAI ENGINE

The Financial Services sector is executed through the CAI Engine under strict governance, evidence, and human oversight constraints.

The CAI Engine enforces governance and execution constraints but does not replace financial, legal, or organizational authority.
