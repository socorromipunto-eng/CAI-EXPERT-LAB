Purpose

The purpose of this document is to define the Government sector as a formal, independent, first-class sector within the CAI USA framework.
Government environments are foundational to national governance, public administration, national security, economic stability, and the delivery of essential public services. Cyber incidents affecting this sector may compromise public trust, disrupt democratic processes, degrade national capabilities, and cascade across multiple critical infrastructure sectors.

This document establishes the authoritative scope, assumptions, constraints, and operational posture required to apply the Cybersecurity Artificial Intelligence Framework coherently within Government environments while preserving accountability, continuity of governance, legal integrity, and regulatory neutrality.

Sector Scope

The Government sector under CAI USA encompasses systems, environments, and operational domains operated or managed by federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial government entities.

This includes, but is not limited to:
Government enterprise IT environments
Citizen-facing digital services and portals
Identity, credential, and access management platforms
Inter-agency data exchange systems
Financial management, taxation, and benefits systems
Regulatory, licensing, and enforcement platforms
National security–adjacent but non-military systems
Government-operated cloud and hybrid environments
Supporting infrastructure that enables public administration and governance

This sector definition intentionally avoids defining regulatory compliance, accreditation, authorization to operate (ATO), or legal mandates, which remain external to CAI.

Sector Assumptions

Government environments operate under strong accountability, continuity, transparency, and legal constraints.
Operational decisions are subject to statutory authority, policy mandates, and public oversight.
Human authority is mandatory for actions with legal, civil, or national consequence.
Automation must be deterministic, explainable, auditable, and reversible.
Cybersecurity actions must not undermine legal processes, public trust, or institutional legitimacy.
Evidence generation, retention, and traceability are mandatory due to legal, investigative, and oversight requirements.
Changes to systems are governed by formal approval, documentation, and review processes.

Operational Characteristics

Government systems are highly heterogeneous, long-lived, and interconnected across agencies and jurisdictions.
Legacy systems often coexist with modern cloud-native platforms.
Interoperability and data sharing are operational necessities but introduce complex trust boundaries.
Downtime tolerance varies but may be extremely low for critical public services.
Incident response must preserve evidence, chain of custody, and decision accountability.
Public transparency, legal defensibility, and oversight obligations shape operational response.

Threat and Risk Context

Threats relevant to the Government sector include disruption of public services, compromise of citizen data, manipulation of identity and trust systems, degradation of situational awareness, influence operations, exploitation of inter-agency trust relationships, and long-term persistent access by advanced adversaries.

Threat modeling prioritizes consequence, institutional impact, and erosion of public trust over exploit novelty or frequency.
Assumed breach is handled conservatively, emphasizing containment, observation, evidence preservation, and governance-aligned response.

Relationship to CAI CORE

This sector definition inherits all lifecycle stages, principles, execution semantics, integrity governance, contamination prevention rules, and evolution controls defined in CAI CORE.
The CAI seven-stage lifecycle is applied without modification.
CAI CORE taxonomy, operational state model, standards governance, and integrity protections remain authoritative.
This document does not redefine CAI CORE concepts.

Relationship to CAI ENGINE

The Government sector is executed through the CAI Engine under strict governance enforcement, safety gating, evidence generation, and mandatory human authority constraints.
Automation and orchestration are bounded by policy enforcement, approval thresholds, and legal accountability requirements.
The CAI Engine enforces execution constraints but does not override governmental authority, statutory mandates, or legal decision-making.

Relationship to CAI USA Baseline

This sector definition extends the CAI USA baseline without redefining it.
It operates consistently with CAI-USA-Definition, CAI-USA-CCES, CAI-USA-Telemetry-and-Data-Model, CAI-USA-Secure-by-Design-Controls, CAI-USA-AI-Governance-and-Assurance, CAI-USA-Threat-Model-Integration, and CAI-USA-Traceability-Matrix.

Sector-specific documents refine assumptions and constraints while preserving USA baseline semantics.

Explicit Non-Claims

This document does not assert compliance with NIST, CISA, CMMC, FedRAMP, FISMA, DOJ, DHS, or any other authority.
It does not certify systems, authorize operational changes, grant authority to operate, or replace legal or regulatory processes.
It defines a cybersecurity operational framework only.
