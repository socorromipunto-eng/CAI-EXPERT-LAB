This document defines the Emergency Services sector profile under the CAI USA baseline and does not assert compliance, certification, accreditation, or regulatory approval.

Purpose

The purpose of this document is to define the Emergency Services sector as a formal, independent, first-class sector within the CAI USA framework.
Emergency Services environments are directly responsible for life-safety operations, crisis response, and continuity of essential public services. Cyber incidents in this sector may result in loss of life, delayed response, or systemic degradation of public safety capabilities.

This document establishes the authoritative scope, assumptions, constraints, and operational posture required to apply CAI coherently within Emergency Services environments while preserving CAI CORE semantics, USA baseline governance, and strict non-claim positioning.

Sector Scope

The Emergency Services sector under CAI USA encompasses systems, environments, and operational domains involved in emergency response coordination, dispatch, communications, incident management, and life-safety support.

This includes, but is not limited to:

Public Safety Answering Points (PSAPs)
Emergency communications and dispatch platforms
Incident command and control systems
Emergency medical, fire, and rescue information systems
Situational awareness, mapping, and resource coordination systems
Interconnected IT, communications, and OT-adjacent infrastructure supporting emergency operations

This sector definition intentionally avoids defining regulatory compliance, certification, accreditation, or operational authority, which remain external to CAI.

Sector Assumptions

Emergency Services environments prioritize preservation of human life and responder safety above all other objectives.
Operational decisions are time-sensitive and often made under degraded or crisis conditions.
Human authority remains mandatory for actions affecting emergency operations, dispatch, or responder coordination.
Automation and AI may assist prioritization and situational awareness but must remain governed, explainable, and subordinate to human command.
Cybersecurity actions must not introduce ambiguity, delay, or operational instability during emergencies.
Evidence generation and accountability remain mandatory despite time pressure and crisis conditions.

Operational Characteristics

Emergency Services systems exhibit strong interdependencies between communications, data, and human decision-making.
Availability and resilience requirements are extremely high, with minimal tolerance for outage or degradation.
Operations must continue during natural disasters, cyber incidents, and hostile conditions.
Coordination often spans multiple agencies, jurisdictions, and organizations.
Incident response must balance cybersecurity containment with uninterrupted emergency response.

Threat and Risk Context

Threats relevant to the Emergency Services sector include disruption of emergency communications, denial of service affecting dispatch or coordination, manipulation of situational awareness data, compromise of identity or access to emergency systems, and supply-chain compromise of emergency technologies.

Threat modeling prioritizes life-safety impact, operational degradation, and cascading effects over technical novelty.
Assumed breach is handled conservatively, emphasizing continuity, containment, and evidence preservation over aggressive eviction.

Relationship to CAI CORE

This sector definition inherits all lifecycle stages, governance principles, integrity protections, contamination prevention rules, and evolution controls defined in CAI CORE.
The CAI seven-stage lifecycle is applied without modification.
CAI CORE remains authoritative for taxonomy, execution semantics, operational state models, framework integrity, and standards governance.

This document does not redefine CAI CORE concepts and must remain consistent with CAI-Core-Definition, CAI-Core-Architecture, CAI-Core-Operations, CAI-Core-Operational-State-Model, and CAI-Core-Standards-Evolution-and-Governance.

Relationship to CAI ENGINE

The Emergency Services sector is executed through the CAI Engine under strict governance, safety gating, and mandatory human oversight.
Automation and orchestration are bounded by policy enforcement, life-safety constraints, and explicit authorization.
The CAI Engine enforces governance boundaries but does not replace emergency command authority or operational decision-making.

Relationship to CAI USA Baseline

This sector definition extends the CAI USA baseline without redefining it.
It operates alongside CAI-USA-Definition, CAI-USA-CCES, CAI-USA-Telemetry-and-Data-Model, CAI-USA-Secure-by-Design-Controls, CAI-USA-AI-Governance-and-Assurance, CAI-USA-Threat-Model-Integration, and CAI-USA-Traceability-Matrix.

Sector-specific documents refine assumptions and constraints without altering USA baseline semantics.

Explicit Non-Claims

This document does not assert compliance with emergency management authorities, public safety regulations, or operational standards.
It does not certify systems, authorize emergency operations, or replace legal or operational governance.
It defines cybersecurity operational governance only.
