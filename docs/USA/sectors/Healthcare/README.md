This README introduces the Healthcare sector overlay under the CAI USA baseline and does not assert compliance, certification, accreditation, or regulatory approval.

Purpose
The purpose of this README is to establish Healthcare and Public Health as a formal, independent sector within the CAI USA framework. Healthcare environments are treated as first-class sector profiles due to their direct impact on human life, patient safety, clinical continuity, data sensitivity, and the increasing convergence of IT, OT, IoMT, and cyber-physical medical systems. This README defines the rationale for sector specialization and provides the contextual foundation for all subsequent Healthcare sector artifacts.

Sector Rationale
Healthcare environments present unique cybersecurity challenges that cannot be fully addressed through generic IT or critical infrastructure models. Clinical operations depend on continuous availability, trusted data integrity, and predictable system behavior. Disruption, data manipulation, or loss of trust can directly impact patient outcomes. The Healthcare sector therefore requires sector-specific constraints, assumptions, and operational posture while inheriting CAI Core and CAI USA baseline semantics.

Sector Scope
The Healthcare sector under CAI USA encompasses healthcare delivery organizations, clinical environments, supporting IT systems, medical device ecosystems, health information systems, research environments, and interconnected operational platforms whose compromise could impact patient safety, care delivery, public health operations, or institutional trust. This includes hospitals, clinics, laboratories, medical imaging systems, electronic health records, identity and access systems, connected medical devices, and supporting infrastructure. This sector definition intentionally avoids defining regulatory compliance, certification, or authorization processes, which remain external to CAI.

Relationship to Legal and Regulatory Frameworks
Healthcare environments in the United States operate under multiple legal and regulatory regimes, including health data protection and patient safety obligations. CAI does not certify compliance with HIPAA, HITECH, FDA, HHS, or any other authority. Instead, CAI provides an operational cybersecurity framework designed to be interoperable with those regimes by embedding accountability, evidence generation, access control discipline, integrity protection, and governed AI usage as foundational principles inherited from CAI CORE and the CAI USA baseline.

Operational Characteristics
Healthcare systems commonly exhibit heterogeneous technologies, legacy platforms, long device lifecycles, and limited maintenance windows. Clinical workflows are tightly coupled to system availability and data accuracy. Human operators, clinicians, biomedical engineers, and IT personnel interact continuously with digital systems. Cybersecurity actions must preserve clinical continuity, operator trust, and patient safety while supporting investigation, accountability, and recovery.

Risk and Impact Context
Healthcare cybersecurity risk is primarily consequence-driven rather than frequency-driven. Threats that degrade availability, manipulate clinical data, disrupt device behavior, or undermine trust in medical systems can result in direct patient harm. Assumed breach handling prioritizes containment, monitoring, and safety preservation over aggressive remediation. Evidence preservation and accountability are mandatory due to legal, ethical, and investigative requirements.

Relationship to CAI CORE
The Healthcare sector inherits all lifecycle stages, principles, integrity controls, and governance rules defined in CAI CORE. The seven-stage CAI lifecycle is applied without modification. CAI CORE remains authoritative for taxonomy, execution semantics, AI governance principles, contamination prevention, and standards evolution.

Relationship to CAI ENGINE
Healthcare sector operations are executed through the CAI Engine under safety-aware constraints and mandatory human oversight. Automation and AI-assisted analytics are permitted for analysis and decision support but are prohibited from autonomous execution of actions that could impact patient safety, clinical continuity, or device behavior. The CAI Engine enforces governance and execution boundaries without overriding clinical or organizational authority.

Relationship to CAI USA Baseline
This Healthcare sector overlay extends the CAI USA baseline without redefining it. It operates in conjunction with CAI-USA-CCES, CAI-USA-Telemetry-and-Data-Model, CAI-USA-AI-Governance-and-Assurance, CAI-USA-Secure-by-Design-Controls, and CAI-USA-Threat-Model-Integration. Sector-specific documents refine assumptions and constraints but do not alter baseline semantics.

Non-Claims
This README does not assert compliance with HIPAA, HHS, FDA, DOJ, DHS, CISA, NIST, or any other authority. It does not certify systems, authorize operational changes, or replace regulatory, clinical, or safety processes. It defines a cybersecurity operational framework only.
